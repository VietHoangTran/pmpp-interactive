# Chapter 20 — Large language models

- **Book pages:** 477–512  **PDF pages:** 501–536 (pdf = book + 24)
- **In one paragraph:** The chapter (credited "with special contributions from Juan Gómez-Luna", p. 477) explains what a decoder-only transformer computes and then concentrates on the one part the book treats as computationally new: the attention head. It builds (1) a baseline row-wise softmax CUDA kernel with the causal mask fused in (Fig. 20.4), (2) the algebra of KV caching, showing that each decode step only needs one new row of Q, K, V, S, P and O, (3) a complete single-head FlashAttention-2-style forward kernel with shared-memory tiles for K^T, S/P and V, register tiles for Q and O, online softmax rescaling, and warp-shuffle data exchange (Figs. 20.8–20.16), and (4) a back-of-envelope analysis of arithmetic intensity and KV-cache memory under batching, speculative decoding, MQA, GQA, MLA, PagedAttention and MoE. After it a reader can re-implement the softmax kernel and the fused flash-attention kernel, reason about why prefill is compute-bound and decode is memory-bandwidth-bound, and size a KV cache. The chapter contains **no measured timings or speedups** and no code for KV caching, batching, quantization or sampling.

Conventions in these notes: `[inference]` marks something I derived that the book does not state; `[checked]` marks a number or claim I re-derived numerically and found consistent with the book. Everything else is taken from the page cited.

## Concepts

### Chapter opening (pp. 477–478)
- Before Google researchers introduced Transformers [1] in 2017, language tasks were mostly statistical language modelling pioneered by IBM [2]; Google's BERT (2018) was Transformer-based and set new results; the Transformer has since been the backbone of neural NLP (p. 477).
- Footnote 1 (p. 477): in this chapter "architecture" means neural-network architecture, not hardware architecture as elsewhere in the book.
- LLMs: orders of magnitude larger than BERT; named to distinguish them from earlier language models; *multi-modal models* are trained on and used for visual, audio and other data such as scientific data (p. 477).
- Core idea: predict the next *token* (word, sub-word or character) given the previous tokens. In chatbots such as ChatGPT [3] the previous tokens are the *context* = system prompt + user input + tokens already generated (pp. 477–478).
- Size language used: "billions of parameters (i.e., tens of layers with hundreds of millions of parameters each)" (p. 478).
- *Attention* is the mechanism that maintains the context and picks out the parts most relevant to the next token (p. 478).
- Footnote 2 (p. 478): "accurate" is used loosely to mean correct/valid/useful. Footnote 3 (p. 478): alternatives to transformers are being explored: *state-space models* (Mamba [4]), convolutional models (Hyena [5]) and hybrids, which may need less memory.

### §20.1 Transformer architecture (pp. 478–482)
Kept brief on purpose (the course reader knows this).
- The book frames attention as resembling a classification step: `softmax` turns similarities into probabilities, and the probability given to each earlier token is its relative importance for producing the next token (p. 478).
- Original transformer: *encoder* layers contextualize ("mix") input tokens into floating-point *embedding vectors*; *decoder* layers consume them and emit tokens one at a time, also taking their own recent output, hence *auto-regressive* (p. 478). *Discriminative* tasks (classification) need no decoder; *decoder-only* models do *generative* tasks and are what LLMs use; the model is executed many times, each time producing likelihoods for the next token (pp. 478–479).
- Components of Fig. 20.1, numbered as in the figure (pp. 479–481):
  1. **Tokenizer**: text to *tokens*, i.e. integer IDs for a character or short character sequence. The book's simplified account: IDs are assigned as new text fragments are met during training and the fragment-to-ID map is "part of the outcome of training"; at inference the map is a lookup. Strictly, tokens are the text fragments and token IDs the integers; the book uses the two terms interchangeably (pp. 479–480).
  2. **Embedding**: lookup of each token ID in an embedding table to get a floating-point vector; values are learned. Reason given: gradient-descent training needs differentiable functions and continuous values (p. 480).
  3. **Positional encoding**: adds order information to the embedding vectors so that attention knows position and proximity (p. 480).
  - The embeddings of the whole conversation so far are stacked into a matrix, one token per row, in order of generation; this is the input of the first transformer layer (p. 480).
  4. **Transformer layers**: each = multi-head attention sub-layer, Add & Norm sub-layer, feed-forward sub-layer, second Add & Norm (p. 480).
  5. **Multi-head attention**: a parallel array of *attention heads*; each linearly projects the embeddings by matrix multiplication (learned matrices, described as a coordinate transformation that makes later similarity comparison more effective), then a second matrix multiplication measures similarity between each token and all tokens generated before it. Head outputs are concatenated and usually linearly transformed (p. 480).
  7. **Add & Norm**: the addition is a *residual connection* that keeps information from the sub-layer's input; the normalization stabilizes and improves convergence, using mean μ and standard deviation σ of the vector's elements and learned γ, β. Figure formula: `LayerNorm(x) = (x − μ)/σ · γ + β` (pp. 480–481, Fig. 20.1).
  6. **Feed-forward**: typically two linear transformations with a ReLU between (footnote 4 points to Appendix B for ReLU/activation). The book's intuition: attention produces similarity-weighted combinations of earlier token embeddings; feed-forward "adds semantic meaning" to them (p. 481).
  8. **Un-embedding layer**: converts the last layer's embeddings into a probability distribution over tokens, used to produce the next output token (p. 481). Nothing more is said about how the token is chosen.
- *Model depth* = number of transformer layers (p. 481). The attention heads, which do large matrix multiplications, are called the most computationally expensive components and are the focus of the rest of the chapter (pp. 481, 482).
- Three "model dimensions" (p. 481): (1) depth; (2) the number of elements in each embedding vector, which the book calls the *head dimension*; (3) the number of *heads* per attention sub-layer. The book states that model size in parameters "is the product of the three model dimensions" and can reach billions or even trillions. (See "Unclear in the PDF" on this simplification.)
- *Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)*: retrieved documents are concatenated with the terse user input to form a much larger context (p. 481).
- Training note (p. 482): the dominant computation in training is large matrix–matrix multiplication in attention heads and feed-forward sub-layers, which can use the Chapter 15 optimizations.

### §20.2 Multi-head attention (pp. 482–486)
- Heads specialize during training in different dependency types: positional (distance), semantic (synonyms, topics), contextual (articles to names), syntactic (verb–object). Their weight matrices are initialized differently; the book attributes non-overlap to gradient descent plus dropout and layer normalization (p. 482).
- GPU-relevant statement: heads are independent, "so they can be easily concurrently computed over multiple thread blocks"; they are identical in design, so the chapter designs one head (p. 482).
- **Data and shapes** (pp. 482–483):
  - `X`: N×d input matrix, one row per token generated so far (system prompt, user input, system augmentation, output tokens). For layer 1 the rows come from positional encoding; for later layers X is the previous layer's output.
  - `N` = *sequence length* = *context length*; grows as a chat proceeds. *Causality policy*: the current token depends only on earlier tokens (p. 482).
  - `d` = head dimension = elements per embedding vector. Head dimension and number of heads are called the *hidden dimensions* (internal, not exposed to users) (pp. 482–483).
  - `W_Q, W_K, W_V`: d×d learned matrices (footnote 5, p. 483: assumed square for simplicity; in general d_x×d_q, d_x×d_k, d_x×d_v, with d_q = d_k forced by QK^T).
  - Step 1 (linear projection): `Q = X·W_Q`, `K = X·W_K`, `V = X·W_V`, each N×d; each output element is a linear combination of the d elements of the same row of X (p. 483).
- **Step 2, Eq. (20.1)** (p. 483): `O = softmax(Q·K^T / √d + M) · V`.
  - `S = Q·K^T` is N×N; element (i, j) is the inner product of projected token i (row i of Q) with projected token j (row j of K). The book describes it as cosine similarity when both vectors are unit length, "which can be enforced with the normalization mechanism"; hence S is a similarity map (p. 484).
  - Scaling `1/√d`: applied to every element of S so dot products do not grow with head dimension; too-large values saturate softmax and give vanishing gradients in training [1]. Omitted from Fig. 20.2 and the surrounding discussion, included in the CUDA code (p. 483 caption, p. 484).
  - Mask `M`: adds −∞ to every element whose column index is greater than its row index; enforces causality (p. 484).
- **Softmax, Eq. (20.2)** (p. 484): with logit `l_{r,c}` = element (r, c) of `QK^T + M`,
  `p_{r,c} = e^{l_{r,c}} / Σ_{j=1..N} e^{l_{r,j}} = e^{l_{r,c} − m_r} / Σ_{j=1..N} e^{l_{r,j} − m_r}`, where `m_r = max_j l_{r,j}`.
  - Reason for subtracting `m_r`: `e^{l}` can overflow; multiplying numerator and denominator by `e^{−m_r}` leaves p unchanged (p. 484).
  - Masked elements give `e^{−∞−m_r} = 0`, so `P = softmax(QK^T + M)` is lower-triangular: p_{r,c} = 0 for r < c. Each row r of P is a probability distribution over tokens 0..r (p. 484).
- Final step: `O = P·V` (N×N times N×d gives N×d). Each row of O is a probability-weighted sum of the V rows of the tokens up to and including that one (the book says "previously generated tokens"). O then passes through Add & Norm and feed-forward to become the next attention sub-layer's X (p. 485).
- **Two inference phases** (pp. 485–486, Fig. 20.3):
  - *Summarization phase*: system prompt, user input and augmentation (e.g. RAG) tokens form X of layer 1; every layer runs the full matrices of Fig. 20.2 and feeds the next; the un-embedding layer emits the first output token.
  - *Autoregression phase* = *decoding phase* = *generation phase*: the emitted token goes back through tokenizer/embedding, its vector is appended as a new row of X, and all layers run again; repeat until an end-of-sequence (EOS) token.
  - Toy example: whole words as tokens, no system prompt. Prompt "The title of this book is Programming" = 7 tokens. Summarization emits "Massively"; generation iteration 1 emits "Parallel", iteration 2 "Processors", iteration 3 EOS. In this baseline each layer recomputes K, V, Q, QK^T and O every time (p. 486).

### §20.3 Implementing attention in CUDA (pp. 486–488)
- Operation inventory for one head (p. 486): two matrix multiplications (QK^T and P·V), one element-wise scaling, one matrix addition (M), and the softmax. The matrix multiplications can be custom kernels or GEMM calls in libraries such as cuBLAS; the `1/√d` scaling can be folded into a custom QK^T kernel or passed as the scaling factor α of cuBLAS's GEMM API. The book says the only new piece is softmax, shows only that, and leaves the rest as an exercise.
- **Kernel decomposition of the baseline** [inference from pp. 486, 488, 492, 497]: (a) projection GEMMs for Q, K, V; (b) GEMM `S = α·Q·K^T` with α = 1/√d; (c) `softmax_kernel` (Fig. 20.4) which also applies the mask; (d) GEMM `O = P·V`. S and P (both N×N) are materialized in global memory between kernels, which is exactly what §20.5 removes.
- **Fusion**: Fig. 20.4 folds the addition of M into the softmax kernel, saving one pass over S and raising arithmetic intensity (p. 486).
- **Data layout**: `S` and `P` are N×N row-major (`S[row*N + col]`); `D` is an N-vector of row denominators (Fig. 20.4, p. 487).
- **Launch configuration** (pp. 486–487): 1D grid with `gridDim.x = N`, one thread block per row of S (`blockIdx.x` = row index). The text says `gridDim.y` and `gridDim.z` "are 0" (see Unclear). Limitation: N cannot exceed the maximum number of blocks in x; fix suggested is an outer loop in the kernel so blocks iterate over rows. Each block is 1D with `BLOCK_SIZE` threads, a compile-time constant; choose it to minimize the iterations each block needs for its row while maximizing SM occupancy.
- **What each thread computes**: thread t of block r handles columns t, t+BLOCK_SIZE, t+2·BLOCK_SIZE, ... of row r (thread coarsening). Three phases: row max (thread-private max, then block-wide `cub::BlockReduce` with `cub::Max()`), row sum of `exp(S − max)` (thread-private sum, then `BlockReduce` with `cub::Sum()`), then write `P`. The causal mask is implemented purely by loop bounds: the max and sum loops stop at `idx <= blockIdx.x`, so masked columns are never read (p. 488).
- `BlockReduce` returns the result only to the thread with `threadIdx.x == 0`; that thread publishes it through a `__shared__` scalar (`max_or_sum`), followed by `__syncthreads()`, and then all threads read it (p. 488). The book describes both phases as "reduction with thread coarsening" from Chapter 10.
- The denominator is written to `D` "for reuse during training" (p. 488).
- Claimed benefit: no global barrier (no separate kernel for adding M); only block-level `__syncthreads()` barriers (p. 488).

### §20.4 KV caching (pp. 488–492)
- Inefficiency of the baseline: every iteration performs **five matrix multiplications per transformer layer** (p. 488) ([inference] the five in Fig. 20.2: X·W_Q, X·W_K, X·W_V, Q·K^T, P·V), and N can reach "tens of thousands of tokens or more", yet X only gains one row per decode iteration.
- **What changes from iteration i (N rows) to i+1 (N+1 rows)** (p. 489, Fig. 20.5; indices 0-based, so the new row is row N):
  - X: one new bottom row (the new token's embedding).
  - Q, K, V: one new bottom row each, a vector–matrix product of the new X row with W_Q, W_K, W_V.
  - QK^T: becomes (N+1)×(N+1). Any element (r, c) with r < N and c < N is unchanged because it depends only on old rows of Q and K. The new row N is a vector–matrix product of the new Q row with K^T. The new column N is entirely masked (the book says "all zero elements" because of the causality masking) except element (N, N), which belongs to the new row.
  - softmax(QK^T): rows 0..N−1 are unchanged, because their new element is masked and contributes nothing to the row's normalization ("do not dilute any of the probabilities") (p. 490).
  - O: rows 0..N−1 unchanged; new row = new row of P times the new V (vector–matrix) (p. 490).
- Consequence: per decode iteration an attention sub-layer receives one new row of X and must deliver one new row of O, but it still needs **all** of K and V. Storing them for reuse ("memoizing") is the *KV cache* (p. 490). Footnote 6: "KV" is not the key-value-store term, though analogous: the output is a weighted sum of values, weighted by how close the query is to the keys [1].
- **Per-token computation with a KV cache** (Fig. 20.6, p. 490–491): `Q' = X'·W_Q`, `K' = X'·W_K`, `V' = X'·W_V` (each 1×d, three vector–matrix products); append K' and V' to the cached K and V; `S' = Q'·K^T` (1×(N+1)); `P' = softmax(S')`; `O' = P'·V` (1×d). No mask appears in Fig. 20.6 ([inference] the newest token may attend to every cached token, so none is needed).
- Phases with KV caching (p. 491, Fig. 20.7): the summarization phase is also called the *prefill phase* because every layer fills its KV cache with its initial K and V; each layer has its own KV cache. In generation, the last output token gives one new embedding vector for layer 1; each layer computes Q', K', V', appends K', V', computes O' and sends it through the rest of the layer to produce the input vector of the next layer.
- **Performance characterization** (pp. 491–492):
  - Prefill: one transformer pass over many tokens, several large matrix–matrix multiplications (GEMMs); "typically compute-bound (high arithmetic intensity)" and can reach high GPU utilization. Flash attention (§20.5) speeds it further by cutting global-memory bandwidth use in the transitions between the GEMMs and softmax.
  - Generation: one pass per output token, mostly vector–matrix multiplications (GEMVs); "typically memory bandwidth bound (low arithmetic intensity)", so it under-utilizes GPU compute. §20.6 gives techniques to raise its arithmetic intensity (batching, speculative decoding).
- The book shows **no code** for the KV-cache data structure, the append, or a decode-phase kernel.

### §20.5 Flash attention (pp. 492–504)
**Motivation (pp. 492–493).**
- A separate softmax kernel (Fig. 20.4) implies global barriers at kernel boundaries and several loads/stores of whole matrices from/to global memory; the book calls this a major bottleneck that the flash attention formulation [6] alleviates.
- Flash attention reformulates a head so its operations can be reordered, fused into one kernel and tiled. Each thread block takes a horizontal panel (contiguous rows) of Q, the entire K^T and the entire V, and produces a horizontal panel of O. It walks K^T and V tile by tile (a vertical panel = contiguous columns of K^T, a horizontal panel of V per step), keeping the intermediate S and P tiles only in on-chip memory. All blocks run in parallel with no global barrier (pp. 492–493).
- It is an **exact** reformulation ("mathematically identical"), usable for training (forward and backward) and inference (forward). The chapter covers the forward pass only. Book's remark: the backward pass is conceptually simpler (no softmax rescaling) but needs five matrix multiplications versus two in the forward pass; see [6] (p. 493).
- Why tiling is harder than for matmul: Eq. (20.2) needs the row maximum `m_r` and the row sum `D_r = Σ_{j=1..N} e^{l_{r,j} − m_r}` before any `p` can be produced, so a naive softmax needs at least one pass over the row to get max and sum and another to compute P, and needs a global barrier so the whole of `QK^T + M` exists first (p. 493).
- Global barrier options in CUDA (p. 493): end the kernel and launch another, or keep a persistent grid and use the grid-level cooperative-groups synchronization API to avoid kernel-call overhead. But good grid configurations for matmul differ from those for softmax, which is a reason the baseline uses separate kernels. Either way, multiple passes over rows of S cost global-memory traffic that limits throughput.

**The online-softmax algebra (pp. 493–495).**
- Partial denominator over a subset A of column positions of row r, Eq. (20.3): `D_{r,A} = Σ_{j∈A} e^{l_{r,j} − m_{r,A}}`, where `m_{r,A}` is the max over A only. When A is the whole row, `D_{r,A} = D_r`.
- Composition rule for disjoint A and B, Eq. (20.4) [7]:
  `D_{r,A∪B} = D_{r,A}·e^{m_{r,A} − m_{r,A∪B}} + D_{r,B}·e^{m_{r,B} − m_{r,A∪B}}`, with `m_{r,A∪B} = max(m_{r,A}, m_{r,B})`.
  Derivation: split the sum over A∪B into sums over A and B (valid only when A and B share no elements), then factor `e^{m_{r,A} − m_{r,A∪B}}` and `e^{m_{r,B} − m_{r,A∪B}}` out of each. So partial sums can be computed independently (potentially in parallel) and merged as long as each carries its max (p. 494). In the kernel the subsets are tiles of a row of S, which are disjoint.
- Flash attention [6] applies the same idea to O. Partial output, Eq. (20.5): `o_{r,c,A} = Σ_{j∈A} p_{r,j,A}·v_{j,c} = (1/D_{r,A})·Σ_{j∈A} e^{l_{r,j} − m_{r,A}}·v_{j,c}`, where `p_{r,j,A}` is the "intermediate" probability using only A's max and denominator (p. 494).
- Composition rule for O, Eq. (20.6) (pp. 494–495):
  `o_{r,c,A∪B} = (D_{r,A}·e^{m_{r,A} − m_{r,A∪B}} / D_{r,A∪B})·o_{r,c,A} + (D_{r,B}·e^{m_{r,B} − m_{r,A∪B}} / D_{r,A∪B})·o_{r,c,B}`.
  Derivation steps as printed: write `o_{r,c,A∪B}` with `e^{l − m_{A∪B}}/D_{A∪B}`; split over A and B; insert `+m_A − m_A` (resp. `+m_B − m_B`) in the exponent; multiply and divide by `D_A` (resp. `D_B`); regroup into `p_{r,j,A}` and `p_{r,j,B}`; recognize `o_{r,c,A}`, `o_{r,c,B}`. Meaning: each partial o was normalized with its own subset's D and m, so both must be rescaled before merging. The rule holds for any subsets but is most useful when a subset is a contiguous segment of a P row produced from tiles of Q and K^T (p. 495).
- [inference, checked] The code does not keep `o` normalized. Its register value is the un-normalized accumulator `Õ = D_{r,A}·o_{r,c,A} = Σ_{j∈A} e^{l_{r,j} − m_{r,A}} v_{j,c}`. Merge in code: `Õ ← Õ·e^{m_old − m_new} + Σ_{j∈B} e^{l_{r,j} − m_new} v_{j,c}`, `D ← D·e^{m_old − m_new} + Σ_{j∈B} e^{l_{r,j} − m_new}`, with one division `Õ/D` at the very end (Fig. 20.16 line 6). This is Eq. (20.6) multiplied through by `D_{r,A∪B}`; the new tile's terms are computed directly against `m_new`, which is why the book says they need no rescaling (p. 503). A pure-Python emulation of this recurrence (N = 64, tiles of 32, causal mask) matched reference attention to 1e-15.

**Kernel design (pp. 495–497, Figs. 20.8, 20.9).**
- Scope: forward pass of **one head**; the whole grid works on that head. "In a more complete implementation, multiple heads can be computed in the same kernel by launching additional dimensions of thread blocks in the grid." The same tiling applies to the summarization phase of inference (p. 495).
- Precision: the listing uses 32-bit floats; state-of-the-art implementations typically use 16-bit (half) or even 8-bit floating point and benefit from tensor cores (p. 495).
- **Data layout**: `Q`, `K`, `V` are N×d, row-major in global memory, passed as `const float*`; `out_O` is N×d; `out_D` is an N-vector of final softmax denominators, needed for training only (pp. 495–496). Host code (allocation, initialization, launch) is left as Exercise 1.
- **Constants in the listing** (Fig. 20.9 lines 1–7, p. 496) `[device: values chosen for the example, not tied to a GPU model]`: `WARP_SIZE 32`, `BLOCK_SIZE 512`, `N_WARPS = BLOCK_SIZE/WARP_SIZE` (= 16), `B_r 32`, `B_r_warp = B_r/N_WARPS` (= 2), `B_c 32`, `d 128`. Derived in the kernel: `T_r = N/B_r` (number of Q/O panels), `T_c = N/B_c` (number of K^T/V tiles), `d_size = d > WARP_SIZE ? d/WARP_SIZE : 1` (= 4).
- **Launch configuration**: 1D grid (x only). Each block produces one horizontal panel of O of size `B_r × d`. The outer loop `for (i = blockIdx.x; i < T_r; i += gridDim.x)` lets a grid with fewer blocks than panels cover all `T_r` panels: each pass of the loop, the grid collectively produces `gridDim.x` panels (p. 497). [inference] Block size must equal `BLOCK_SIZE` (512 threads, 16 warps), since `B_r_warp` and all row indexing assume `N_WARPS` warps per block.
- Choosing `B_r`: maximize data reuse in on-chip memory, limited by the on-chip memory available and by the number of thread blocks needed to fully use the GPU (p. 497). Choosing `B_c`: set to minimize the number of block-level iterations and hence merges of the O tile; limited by shared memory; restricted to multiples of `WARP_SIZE` to avoid boundary checks and padding (p. 499). `d` is assumed a multiple of `WARP_SIZE` (p. 498).
- **Three conceptual steps per Q panel** (p. 497): (1) `B_r×d` panel of Q times all of K^T gives a `B_r×N` panel of S; (2) softmax turns it into a `B_r×N` panel of P; (3) that times all of V gives the `B_r×d` tile of O. A naive version would be three kernels writing and reading S and P in global memory (low arithmetic intensity plus kernel-launch overhead). Eq. (20.6) lets the three be fused and done piecemeal in on-chip memory so S and P are never materialized in global memory.
- **Shared-memory / register tiling scheme** (p. 497):
  - Registers (per-thread local arrays): tiles of **Q** and **O**, plus per-row `m_i` (running max) and `D_i` (running denominator).
  - Shared memory: `KT_j` (current tile of K^T, d rows × B_c columns, flattened and padded), `S_i[B_r][B_c]` (S tile, overwritten in place with the P tile), `V_j[B_c][d]` (current tile of V).
  - Declarations (Fig. 20.9 lines 13–15): `__shared__ float KT_j[B_c*d+((B_c*d)>>LOG_NUM_BANKS)]; __shared__ float S_i[B_r][B_c]; __shared__ float V_j[B_c][d];`
  - [inference, checked arithmetic] With the listing's constants and 32 banks (`LOG_NUM_BANKS` = 5, not defined in this chapter): 4096+128 + 1024 + 4096 = 9344 floats = 37,376 bytes of shared memory per block, plus the `WarpReduce::TempStorage` objects declared inside two device functions.
  - Per-thread registers (lines 17–19, 22): `O_i[B_r_warp][d_size]`, `D_i[B_r_warp]`, `m_i[B_r_warp]`, `Q_i[B_r_warp][d_size]`; with the constants that is 8 + 2 + 2 + 8 = 20 floats per thread [inference arithmetic]. The book notes the total register need is set by d and B_r, each thread holds only part of a tile, and threads exchange elements through the warp-shuffle API (pp. 497–498).
- **Work distribution inside a block** (p. 498, Fig. 20.8): the block's `B_r×d` Q panel is split into sub-panels of `B_r_warp×d`, one per warp. Within a warp, each row is stored *interleaved* across the 32 lanes: the row is cut into sections of `WARP_SIZE` elements and each section is dealt out one element per lane, so lane L holds columns L, L+32, L+64, ... of each of the warp's rows, i.e. `B_r_warp × (d/WARP_SIZE)` elements of Q per thread. This makes every section load coalesced. `laneIdx()` and `warpIdx()` are the Chapter 10 helpers (`threadIdx.x % WARP_SIZE`, `threadIdx.x / WARP_SIZE`).
- Footnote 7 (p. 498): distributing **Q rows across warps** is the FlashAttention-2 scheme [8]. FlashAttention-1 [6] distributed K and V across warps ("split-K"), which is inefficient because warps must write intermediate results to shared memory, synchronize and reduce. In version 2 warps exchange nothing with each other; all exchanges are among threads of one warp.
- **Two-level iteration** (pp. 498–499):
  - *Block level* (loop over `j`, `T_c = N/B_c` iterations): move left to right over K^T and top to bottom over V, `B_c` columns of K^T and `B_c` rows of V per iteration; load both tiles into shared memory; `__syncthreads()`; run the warp-level loop; `__syncthreads()` again before the next tile.
  - *Warp level* (loop over `ii`, `B_r_warp` iterations): for one row of the warp's Q sub-panel: vector–matrix product with the K^T tile gives a `B_c`-element row segment of S (`compute_S_and_max`); update running max and rescale running denominator (`update_m_and_D`); exponentiate in place to get the P segment and add its sum to the denominator (`compute_P_and_update_D`); rescale the O row and add the vector–matrix product of the P segment with the V tile (`compute_O`).
- The book first explains the kernel with softmax ignored (two chained tiled vector–matrix products accumulating into O across tiles, pp. 498–501), then adds softmax back (pp. 501–503).
- **K^T tile loading** (p. 499): the tile is read from K (N×d, so each key is a row) and stored transposed in shared memory "for efficiency". To avoid shared-memory bank conflicts the array is padded, and every access goes through `addr(x) = x + (x >> LOG_NUM_BANKS)`, attributed to Chapter 16.
- **S segment computation** (p. 500): the K^T tile is treated as sub-panels of `WARP_SIZE` columns, one column per lane. Each lane does a d-long inner product. At step `dd` every lane needs Q element `dd` of the current row, which lives in exactly one lane's registers, so that lane broadcasts it with `__shfl_sync()`. Causality: compare global row and column; if column > row store −∞, otherwise store `scaling × S_ij` (scaling = 1/√d passed as kernel argument).
- Footnote 8 (pp. 500–501): the shuffle broadcasts a Q value from one thread's register to the warp. This matmul uses shared-memory and register tiling but could use more sophisticated Chapter 15 optimizations (hierarchical tiling, tensor cores).
- **Mapping of code variables to the equations** (p. 502): in `update_m_and_D`, `last_m` = `m_{r,A}`, `curr_max_warp` = `m_{r,B}`, updated `m_i[ii]` = `m_{r,A∪B}`, where A = columns processed so far and B = the new tile.
- **End of block work** (p. 503): after all `T_c` tiles the O tile holds the full result although S and P only ever existed as tiles in shared memory. `store_O` divides by the denominator and writes `out_O` and `out_D`.
- The described iteration is "only one of the many possibilities"; for example a different process would be used if tensor cores did the two per-warp matrix products (p. 503).
- **Applicability** (p. 504): the kernel applies to the baseline transformer without KV caching and to the **prefill** phase with KV caching. In the generation phase with KV caching Q, S, P, O become vectors Q', S', P', O', so matrix products become vector–matrix products; to keep a work distribution like Fig. 20.8 one can batch multiple requests (multiple `Q_i`) into one matrix (next section).
- **FlashAttention-3** [9] (p. 504) `[device: NVIDIA Hopper GPU architecture]`: uses (1) the Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA), which asynchronously moves tiles from global to shared memory for the tensor cores without involving compute threads; (2) tensor-core WarpGroup-wide Matrix Multiply Accumulate (WGMMA) instructions, asynchronous unlike earlier architectures; (3) FP8 precision of the tensor cores. With warp specialization it overlaps data movement and computation better and "hides softmax execution with asynchronous block-wise GEMMs". No code is given.

### §20.6 KV cache arithmetic intensity and memory requirement (pp. 504–508)
- Recap: in generation, one new K row and one new V row per token are produced by vector–matrix operations, which have low arithmetic intensity and under-use GPU compute (refers to Chapter 5). *Batching* and *speculative decoding* aim to raise it (p. 504).
- **Batching** (pp. 504–505): common in all DNNs to raise arithmetic (computational) intensity.
  - Worked number: a linear layer with 1024 inputs and 4096 outputs in 16-bit precision has arithmetic intensity ≈ **1 FLOPS/B** (the book's unit: floating-point operations per byte) at batch size 1, and **315 FLOPS/B** at batch size 512. Cause: the weights are reused across input vectors. Footnote 9: this is the theoretical increase; in practice it depends on the tile size used.
  - [inference, checked] The numbers are reproduced by: FLOPs = b × 2 × 1024 × 4096; bytes = weights (1024 × 4096 × 2 B = 8,388,608) + b × (1024 + 4096) × 2 B for inputs and outputs. b = 1 gives 0.9988; b = 512 gives 315.08.
  - LLMs "impose a tradeoff between batch size and context length (i.e., KV cache size)". Queries from many users share the same model weights, so the fully connected layers that produce Q, K, V batch well: batching raises the arithmetic intensity of the QKV projection by a factor equal to the batch size (left half of Fig. 20.17).
  - But each user's KV cache depends on that user's prompt and context, so attention layers "do not benefit from batching": each query needs its own KV cache (right half of Fig. 20.17). Batching is therefore limited by the memory needed for model weights plus KV caches.
- **Memory requirements** (pp. 505–506):
  - Model weights: several billion parameters is typical; 7 billion parameters in a 16-bit format (FP16 or BF16) is about 14 GB. Pointers to [10, 11].
  - The book defines the *hidden dimension* here as (number of attention query heads `h_q`) × (embedding dimension `d`).
  - Eq. (20.7), KV cache bytes **per token**: `KV_cache_size_MHA = 2 × l × h_q × d × p`; `l` = number of transformer layers, `p` = precision in bytes, the 2 covers K and V.
  - Eq. (20.8), total for a batch of `b` sequences of length `N`: `Total_KV_cache_size_MHA = b × N × 2 × l × h_q × d × p`.
  - Worked examples, 16-bit, one sequence of 4096 tokens (p. 506) [checked; the "GB" are binary, 2^30 bytes]:
    - PaLM 2 (Google): 1 × 4096 × 2 × 64 × 8192 × 2 = 8 GB (l = 64, hidden dimension 8192).
    - GPT-3 (OpenAI): 1 × 4096 × 2 × 96 × 12288 × 2 = 18 GB.
    - Llama 2 7B (Meta): 1 × 4096 × 2 × 32 × 4096 × 2 = 2 GB.
  - Conclusion: batching many queries can easily exceed a single GPU's memory, so multi-GPU and multi-node systems are commonly needed for LLM inference.
- **Unequal lengths and in-flight batching** (p. 506): the analysis assumed equal sequence lengths; real users' input and output lengths differ, which the book says may unbalance work across thread blocks and under-use the GPU. Serving systems use *in-flight batching* [12]: run batches of several requests at a time and slot a new request into the batch with the lowest workload as soon as a previous one is served.
- **Arithmetic intensity of MHA in decode** (pp. 506–507), estimated from data movement and operation count [13], one layer, `p = 1`:
  - KV cache read: `2 × h_q × d × N`. Reading Q' and writing O': `2 × h_q × d`. Operations for `(Q'K^T)V`: `2 × N × h_q × d` (softmax ignored).
  - Eq. (20.9): `AI_MHA ≈ (2·N·h_q·d) / (2·h_q·d + 2·h_q·d·N) = N/(1+N) ≈ 1` floating-point operation per byte. Batching does not improve this.
- **Speculative decoding** [14] (pp. 507–508, Fig. 20.18): a model-serving optimization. A tiny *draft* model predicts several consecutive output tokens of a large *target* model; the target model, which has much longer latency, verifies those predictions in parallel as a batch. *Speculation depth* = number of tokens drafted per round (3 in the example). Round 1: draft proposes "Programming", "Massively", "Efficient"; target accepts the first two and rejects "Efficient" (its own token there is "Parallel"); accepted tokens join the input for the next draft round.
  - Why it helps, per the book: it is like batching, so it raises the arithmetic intensity of the linear projections; and it is a special batch in which all entries share a common prefix, so the same KV-cache data for the common tokens is loaded once and reused for all the output tokens instead of once per output token. That reuse is what raises attention's arithmetic intensity (p. 508).
- **Latency versus throughput of batching: not discussed in this chapter.** The chapter argues only in terms of arithmetic intensity, GPU utilization and memory capacity. "Latency" appears twice: the target model's "much longer latency" (p. 507) and the summary's claim that flash attention "reduces the latency of the prefill phase" (p. 510). "Throughput" appears once (global-memory traffic limiting it, p. 493).

### §20.7 Alleviating the memory requirements of the attention mechanism (pp. 508–510)
- Cause of MHA's memory need: every head has its own weight matrices and so its own K and V (p. 508).
- **Multi-query attention (MQA)**: all heads share one K and one V. Eq. (20.10): `Total_KV_cache_size_MQA = b × N × 2 × l × d × p`, a reduction by the number of heads. Eq. (20.11): `AI_MQA ≈ (2·N·h_q·d) / (2·h_q·d + 2·d·N) = N·h_q/(h_q + N) ≈ h_q`, i.e. about `h_q` times MHA's (p. 508).
- **Grouped-query attention (GQA)** [15]: between MHA and MQA, "using a few groups (g_q) of query heads". Eq. (20.12): `Total_KV_cache_size_GQA = b × N × 2 × l × (h_q/g_q) × d × p`. Eq. (20.13): `AI_GQA ≈ (2·N·h_q·d) / (2·h_q·d + 2·(h_q/g_q)·d·N) = N·h_q / (h_q + (h_q/g_q)·N) ≈ g_q` (pp. 508–509). See Unclear for the meaning of `g_q`.
- MHA, GQA and MQA can all use the flash-attention tiling of §20.5; they trade computation, memory and accuracy differently (p. 509).
- **PagedAttention** [12] (p. 509): batch size and context length are still limited by GPU memory, made worse because KV-cache memory is typically over-provisioned for the largest possible input, leaving it fragmented and wasted. PagedAttention, inspired by OS paging, splits the KV cache into equal-size blocks that are loaded into GPU memory (from the larger system memory) when needed for computation.
- **Multi-head Latent Attention (MLA)** [16] (pp. 509–510): low-rank joint compression of K and V; each token's K and V vectors are compressed into one latent vector, across all heads and layers. Eq. (20.14): `Total_KV_cache_size_MLA = b × N × l × d × p`. Eq. (20.15): `AI_MLA ≈ (2·N·h_q·d) / (2·h_q·d + d·N) = 2·N·h_q/(h_q + N) ≈ 2·h_q` (as printed). Projection matrices are learned in training; at inference K and V are decompressed by two up-projection matrices just before use, so only the latent form is kept between iterations and decompression happens only when a head is active in generation.
- **Mixture of Experts (MoE)**, e.g. Mixtral [17] (p. 510): orthogonal scaling technique; a gating mechanism selects a small subset of specialized sub-networks per input, so parameter count can be huge while per-token computation is reduced.

### §20.8 Summary (p. 510)
- Restates the path: naive multi-kernel attention with emphasis on the softmax kernel; KV caching removes redundant computation in generation; flash attention raises arithmetic intensity and cuts prefill latency; memory requirements of KV caching and ways to reduce them when batching. The chapter is positioned as fundamentals, with a very large design space left open.

## Code listings

### Fig. 20.4 — `softmax_kernel` (p. 487)
- **Purpose:** from the already scaled N×N matrix `S = QK^T/√d`, produce `P = softmax(S + M)` and the row denominators `D`, with the causal mask applied implicitly.
- **Signature:** `__global__ void softmax_kernel(float* S, float* D, float* P, int N)`.
- **Launch configuration assumed:** 1D grid with `gridDim.x = N` (block index = row); 1D blocks of `BLOCK_SIZE` threads (compile-time constant, value not given). Not shown in the figure (pp. 486–487).
- **Walkthrough:**
  - Lines 2–4: `typedef cub::BlockReduce<float, BLOCK_SIZE> BlockReduce;`, a `__shared__` `BlockReduce::TempStorage temp_store`, and a `__shared__ float max_or_sum` used to broadcast first the max, then the sum.
  - Line 5: `S_row = &S[blockIdx.x * N]`.
  - Lines 6–9 (max, thread-private part): `max_val_thread = NEG_INFINITY`; loop `for (idx = threadIdx.x; idx <= blockIdx.x; idx += blockDim.x)` keeps the largest `S_row[idx]`.
  - Line 10: `__syncthreads()`. Line 11: `max_val_row = BlockReduce(temp_store).Reduce(max_val_thread, cub::Max())`.
  - Lines 12–14 (broadcast): `if (threadIdx.x == 0) max_or_sum = max_val_row; __syncthreads(); max_val_row = max_or_sum;`.
  - Lines 15–18 (sum, thread-private part): `sum_thread += exp(S_row[idx] - max_val_row)` over the same masked index range.
  - Lines 19–23: `__syncthreads()`, `BlockReduce(temp_store).Reduce(sum_thread, cub::Sum())`, same thread-0 broadcast through `max_or_sum`, `__syncthreads()`, read back into `sum_row`.
  - Lines 24–26 (output): loop over **all** columns `idx < N` with stride `blockDim.x`; `P[blockIdx.x * N + idx] = idx <= blockIdx.x ? exp(S_row[idx] - max_val_row) / sum_row : 0;`.
  - Line 27: `if (threadIdx.x == 0) D[blockIdx.x] = sum_row;`.
- **Lines that need care:**
  - The mask is the loop bound `idx <= blockIdx.x` (column ≤ row) in the max and sum loops; masked entries of S are never read, and the output loop writes explicit zeros for them.
  - The reduction result is only valid in thread 0 (book, p. 488), hence the shared scalar plus two extra `__syncthreads()` per reduction.
  - The same `temp_store` and the same `max_or_sum` are reused for both reductions; the barrier at line 19 separates the last reads of the max (line 14) from thread 0's write of the sum (line 21) [inference].
  - For row r only r+1 elements are live, so blocks for small rows do almost no work and thread t > r contributes `NEG_INFINITY` / 0 to the reductions [inference].
  - `exp(S − max)` is recomputed in the output loop rather than stored.
- **APIs / qualifiers / built-ins:** `__global__`, `__shared__`, `__syncthreads()`, `blockIdx.x`, `threadIdx.x`, `blockDim.x`, `cub::BlockReduce<float, BLOCK_SIZE>` with `TempStorage`, `.Reduce(value, cub::Max())`, `.Reduce(value, cub::Sum())`, `exp`, `NEG_INFINITY` (not defined in the chapter).

### Fig. 20.9 — `flashattention_forward_kernel` (p. 496)
- **Purpose:** fused forward pass of one causal attention head, `O = softmax(scaling·QK^T + M)·V`, never materializing S or P in global memory. Caption cites [8] (FlashAttention-2).
- **Signature:** `__global__ void flashattention_forward_kernel(const float* Q, const float* K, const float* V, const int N, const float scaling, float* out_D, float* out_O)`.
- **Constants (lines 1–7):** `WARP_SIZE 32`, `BLOCK_SIZE 512`, `N_WARPS BLOCK_SIZE / WARP_SIZE`, `B_r 32`, `B_r_warp B_r / N_WARPS`, `B_c 32`, `d 128`.
- **Launch configuration assumed:** 1D grid, any `gridDim.x` (the `i` loop strides by it); blocks of `BLOCK_SIZE` threads [inference: required by the warp-to-row mapping]. `scaling` = 1/√d supplied by the host.
- **Walkthrough:**
  - Lines 10–12: `T_r = N / B_r`, `T_c = N / B_c`, `d_size = d > WARP_SIZE ? d / WARP_SIZE : 1`.
  - Lines 13–15: shared arrays `KT_j` (flattened, padded), `S_i[B_r][B_c]`, `V_j[B_c][d]`.
  - Line 16: `for (int i = blockIdx.x; i < T_r; i += gridDim.x)`, one Q/O panel per pass.
  - Lines 17–21: per-thread `O_i[B_r_warp][d_size]`, `D_i[B_r_warp]`, `m_i[B_r_warp]`; `initialize(O_i, D_i, m_i)` (body left as Exercise 3).
  - Lines 22–24: per-thread `Q_i[B_r_warp][d_size]`; `load_Q(Q, Q_i, i)`.
  - Line 25: `for (int j = 0; j < T_c; j++)`, one K^T/V tile per pass.
  - Lines 27–28: `load_KT_and_V(K, V, KT_j, V_j, j); __syncthreads();`.
  - Lines 29–38: `for (int ii = 0; ii < B_r_warp; ii++)`: `curr_max_warp = compute_S_and_max(Q_i, KT_j, S_i, i, j, ii, scaling);` then `last_m = update_m_and_D(m_i, D_i, curr_max_warp, ii);` then `curr_sum_warp = compute_P_and_update_D(S_i, m_i, D_i, i, j, ii);` then `compute_O(S_i, V_j, O_i, ii, last_m, m_i);`.
  - Line 39: `__syncthreads()` before the next tile overwrites shared memory.
  - Line 42: `store_O(out_O, out_D, O_i, D_i, i, N);` after all tiles.
- **Lines that need care:**
  - Two block-level barriers per tile: after loading (tiles complete before use) and after the warp loop (all warps done before tiles are replaced). Inside the warp loop there is no `__syncthreads()`: warps touch disjoint rows of `S_i`, and K^T/V tiles are read-only.
  - Within a warp, each lane writes one element of the S/P row and then, in `compute_O`, reads all `B_c` elements written by the other lanes. The book adds no explicit barrier for this; the only intervening warp-wide operations are the `WarpReduce` and `__shfl_sync` calls [inference: correctness relies on warp-level ordering].
  - `T_r` and `T_c` use integer division, so N is implicitly a multiple of `B_r` and `B_c` [inference]; the book states only that `B_c` must be a multiple of `WARP_SIZE` and `d` a multiple of `WARP_SIZE` (pp. 498–499).
  - `curr_sum_warp` (line 35) is not used afterwards.
  - `LOG_NUM_BANKS` and `addr()` are not defined in the chapter's listings.
- **APIs / qualifiers / built-ins:** `__global__`, `__shared__`, `__syncthreads()`, `blockIdx.x`, `gridDim.x`, preprocessor constants, multi-dimensional `__shared__` arrays, per-thread local arrays intended to live in registers.

### Fig. 20.10 — `load_Q` (p. 498)
- **Purpose:** load the warp's `B_r_warp × d` sub-panel of Q from global memory into per-thread registers, interleaved by lane.
- **Signature:** `__device__ inline void load_Q(const float* Q, float Q_i[B_r_warp][d_size], int i)`.
- **Walkthrough:** outer loop `ii` over the warp's rows; inner loop `for (int dd = laneIdx(), ddd = 0; dd < d; dd += WARP_SIZE, ddd++)`; body `Q_i[ii][ddd] = Q[(B_r*i+B_r_warp*warpIdx()+ii)*d+dd];`.
- **Care:** global row = `B_r*i + B_r_warp*warpIdx() + ii`; lane L ends up with columns L + 32·ddd. For a fixed `ddd` the 32 lanes read 32 consecutive floats of one row, hence coalesced (p. 498). No bounds checks.
- **APIs:** `__device__ inline`, `laneIdx()`, `warpIdx()` (Chapter 10 helpers).

### Fig. 20.11 — `load_KT_and_V` (p. 499)
- **Purpose:** load tile j of K (transposed) and of V into shared memory.
- **Signature:** `__device__ inline void load_KT_and_V(const float* K, const float* V, float* KT_j, float V_j[B_c][d], int j)`.
- **Walkthrough:** `for (jj = 0; jj < B_c; jj++)` over the tile's tokens; `for (int dd = threadIdx.x; dd < d; dd += blockDim.x)`; body `KT_j[addr(dd * B_c + jj)] = K[(B_c*j+jj) * d + dd];` and `V_j[jj][dd] = V[(B_c * j + jj) * d + dd];`.
- **Care:**
  - Logical layout of `KT_j` is d rows × B_c columns (`dd * B_c + jj`), i.e. K^T; the global read walks along a row of K (one token's key), so consecutive threads read consecutive addresses.
  - `addr(x) = x + (x >> LOG_NUM_BANKS)` inserts one padding slot per `2^LOG_NUM_BANKS` elements. [inference] Without it, consecutive threads (`dd`, `dd+1`) would write addresses `B_c` = 32 apart, all in the same bank if there are 32 banks; with it the stride becomes 33.
  - [inference] With d = 128 and 512 threads per block, only threads 0–127 execute the loop body; the others do nothing in this function.
  - The text (p. 499) calls this function `load_KTV()`; the listing and the kernel call it `load_KT_and_V`.
- **APIs:** `threadIdx.x`, `blockDim.x`, `addr()` padding macro (called `pad(x)` in Chapter 16).

### Fig. 20.12 — `compute_S_and_max` (p. 500)
- **Purpose:** for row `ii` of the warp's sub-panel, compute the `B_c`-element segment of S against the current K^T tile, apply mask and scaling, store it in `S_i`, and return the segment maximum to every lane.
- **Signature:** `__device__ inline float compute_S_and_max(const float Q_i[B_r_warp][d_size], const float* KT_j, float S_i[B_r][B_c], int i, int j, int ii, float scaling)`.
- **Walkthrough:**
  - Lines 2–3: `typedef cub::WarpReduce<float> WarpReduce;` and `__shared__ typename WarpReduce::TempStorage temp_store;`.
  - Line 4: `curr_max = NEG_INFINITY`.
  - Line 5: `for (int jj = laneIdx(); jj < B_c; jj += WARP_SIZE)`: lane L owns tile columns L, L+32, ... (one column each when B_c = 32).
  - Lines 6–10: inner product over `dd = 0..d−1`: `float q = __shfl_sync(0xFFFFFFFF, Q_i[ii][dd / WARP_SIZE], dd % WARP_SIZE);` then `S_ij += q * KT_j[addr(dd * B_c + jj)];`.
  - Lines 11–12: `row = B_r * i + B_r_warp * warpIdx() + ii; col = B_c * j + jj;`.
  - Line 13: `S_ij = row < col ? NEG_INFINITY : scaling * S_ij;`.
  - Line 14: `S_i[B_r_warp * warpIdx() + ii][jj] = S_ij;`. Line 15: update `curr_max`.
  - Lines 17–18: `curr_max_warp = cub::WarpReduce<float>(temp_store).Reduce(curr_max, cub::Max());` then `curr_max_warp = __shfl_sync(0xFFFFFFFF, curr_max_warp, 0);`; return it.
- **Care:**
  - The shuffle fetches Q element `dd` from lane `dd % WARP_SIZE`, slot `dd / WARP_SIZE`, matching the interleaved layout of `load_Q`. One shuffle per `dd`, so d shuffles per S element computed by a lane.
  - The warp's local row in the shared tile is `B_r_warp * warpIdx() + ii`; warps never share a row.
  - Only lane 0 gets the `WarpReduce` result (book, p. 502), so a shuffle from lane 0 broadcasts it.
  - A segment lying wholly above the diagonal returns `NEG_INFINITY` as its max [inference].
- **APIs:** `__shfl_sync(mask, value, srcLane)`, `cub::WarpReduce<float>`, `TempStorage`, `.Reduce(value, cub::Max())`, `laneIdx()`, `warpIdx()`, `addr()`.

### Fig. 20.13 — `compute_O` (p. 501)
- **Purpose:** merge step of Eq. (20.6) for one row: rescale the running O row for the new max, then add the product of the P segment (stored in `S_i`) with the V tile.
- **Signature:** `__device__ inline void compute_O(const float S_i[B_r][B_c], const float V_j[B_c][d], float O_i[B_r_warp][d_size], int ii, float last_m, float* m_i)`.
- **Walkthrough:** loop `for (int dd = laneIdx(), ddd = 0; dd < d; dd += WARP_SIZE, ddd++)` over the O columns this lane owns; line 3 `O_i[ii][ddd] *= exp(last_m - m_i[ii]);`; lines 4–7 `O_ij += S_i[B_r_warp*warpIdx()+ii][jj] * V_j[jj][dd];` for `jj = 0..B_c−1`; line 8 `O_i[ii][ddd] += O_ij;`.
- **Care:**
  - Rescale before accumulate. The factor is `e^{m_{r,A} − m_{r,A∪B}}`; it is 1 when the max did not change. The book calls it "the numerator of the first addend" of Eq. (20.6) (p. 503).
  - The new contribution is not rescaled because `compute_P_and_update_D` already exponentiated against the updated max (p. 503).
  - Each lane reads the entire P segment (all `jj`), including elements written by other lanes of its warp.
  - O is interleaved across lanes exactly like Q (lane L owns columns L + 32·ddd).
- **APIs:** `exp`, `laneIdx()`, `warpIdx()`.

### Fig. 20.14 — `update_m_and_D` (p. 502)
- **Purpose:** fold the new segment's max into the running row max, and rescale the running denominator accordingly (the `D_{r,A}·e^{m_{r,A} − m_{r,A∪B}}` term of Eq. 20.4).
- **Signature:** `__device__ inline float update_m_and_D(float* m_i, float* D_i, float curr_max_warp, int ii)`.
- **Walkthrough (whole body):** `last_m = m_i[ii]; D = D_i[ii]; if (m_i[ii] < curr_max_warp) { m_i[ii] = curr_max_warp; D *= exp(last_m - m_i[ii]); } D_i[ii] = D; return last_m;`.
- **Care:** returns the **old** max, which `compute_O` needs for its rescale. Every lane holds its own identical copy of `m_i` and `D_i` because the max and sum are broadcast to the whole warp [inference]. If `m_i` starts at −∞ the first update multiplies D = 0 by `exp(−∞)` = 0, which is harmless [inference].
- **APIs:** `exp`.

### Fig. 20.15 — `compute_P_and_update_D` (p. 503)
- **Purpose:** turn the S segment into the (un-normalized) P segment in place, and add the segment's sum to the running denominator.
- **Signature:** `__device__ inline float compute_P_and_update_D(float S_i[B_r][B_c], float* m_i, float* D_i, int i, int j, int ii)`.
- **Walkthrough:** `WarpReduce` typedef and `__shared__` temp storage (lines 2–3); `curr_sum = 0.f`; loop `for (int jj = laneIdx(); jj < B_c; jj += WARP_SIZE)`: recompute `row`, `col`; line 8 `float P_ij = row < col ? 0 : (exp(S_i[B_r_warp * warpIdx() + ii][jj] - m_i[ii]));`; line 9 write `P_ij` back into the same `S_i` slot; line 10 `curr_sum += P_ij`. Line 12 `cub::WarpReduce<float>(temp_store).Reduce(curr_sum, cub::Sum())`; line 13 broadcast from lane 0 with `__shfl_sync(0xFFFFFFFF, curr_sum_warp, 0)`; line 14 `D_i[ii] += curr_sum_warp;`; return `curr_sum_warp`.
- **Care:**
  - "P" here is `e^{l − m}` with `m` = the already updated running max, not a normalized probability; normalization happens once in `store_O`.
  - The mask is applied a second time (explicit 0) so that masked −∞ entries never reach `exp`.
  - The sum must be broadcast to all lanes because every lane keeps its own `D_i` copy and will divide by it in `store_O`.
- **APIs:** `cub::WarpReduce<float>`, `cub::Sum()`, `__shfl_sync`, `exp`.

### Fig. 20.16 — `store_O` (p. 504)
- **Purpose:** normalize and write the warp's O rows, and the denominators, to global memory.
- **Signature:** `__device__ inline void store_O(float* out_O, float* out_D, const float O_i[B_r_warp][d_size], const float D_i[B_r_warp], int i, int N)`.
- **Walkthrough:** for each `ii`: `row = B_r * i + B_r_warp * warpIdx() + ii`; for `dd = 0..d_size−1`: `col = dd * WARP_SIZE + laneIdx();` and `if (row<N && col<d) out_O[row*d+col] = O_i[ii][dd]/D_i[ii];`; then `if (laneIdx() == 0) out_D[row] = D_i[ii];`.
- **Care:** the division by D happens only here (p. 503). Lanes write consecutive columns for each `dd`, so the store is coalesced [inference]. Only this function has bounds tests. `out_D` holds the denominator relative to the final row max; the max itself is not written.
- **APIs:** `laneIdx()`, `warpIdx()`.

## Relevance to LLM-inference kernels

- **Attention (high relevance).** This is the book's only end-to-end attention kernel. Directly reusable: the causal-mask-by-index trick, max-subtracted softmax, online-softmax recurrence for (m, D, O), the storage plan (Q and O in registers, K^T, S/P and V in shared memory), row-to-warp assignment, lane-interleaved register tiles with shuffle broadcast, padding against bank conflicts, and the barrier placement.
- **matmul / GEMM (medium).** The chapter writes no GEMM; it points to Chapter 15 and cuBLAS. The two products inside the flash kernel are deliberately simple warp-level vector–matrix loops, and footnote 8 says they could use hierarchical tiling or tensor cores. The linear-layer AI example (1 vs. 315 FLOPS/B) and the GEMM-vs-GEMV framing explain why decode is bandwidth-bound.
- **Softmax for sampling (indirect).** Fig. 20.4 is a general row-wise softmax with block-wide max and sum reductions; with the mask bound removed it is the pattern needed for a softmax over vocabulary logits [inference]. The chapter itself never applies it to logits.
- **Quantized ops (little direct relevance).** Only remarks: FP16/BF16 weight storage, 16-bit and 8-bit floating-point attention, FP8 tensor cores on Hopper, precision `p` in the size formulas. Reference [10] has "quantized" in its title but is cited only for weight memory.
- **KV cache (conceptual only).** Exact algebra of what is reused, phase characterization, size formulas, MQA/GQA/MLA/paging; no data structure or kernel.

**What the chapter does NOT cover that an LLM-inference kernel author needs (to be filled by the course):**

- Other layer kernels: layer normalization (only the formula in Fig. 20.1), feed-forward/MLP, activation functions beyond a mention of ReLU, embedding lookup, positional encoding of any specific kind, the un-embedding projection.

## Unclear in the PDF

Suspected errata (read directly from the page, judged by me to be slips):
- p. 487: text says "Both `gridDim.y` and `gridDim.z` are 0" for a 1D grid; unused grid dimensions are normally 1.
- p. 495, first line of the continued Eq. (20.6) derivation: the second sum's exponent is printed `l_{r,j} − m_{r,B}` where the preceding and following lines require `l_{r,j} − m_{r,A∪B}`.
- p. 502: text says line 6 of Fig. 20.14 "computes the term `D_{r,B}·e^{m_{r,B} − m_{r,A∪B}}` of Eq. (20.4)". The code line is `D *= exp(last_m - m_i[ii])` acting on the old denominator, which is the **A** term `D_{r,A}·e^{m_{r,A} − m_{r,A∪B}}`.
- p. 502: text says Fig. 20.15's accumulation "corresponds to the sum Σ_{j∈B} e^{l_{r,j} − m_{r,B}}"; the code subtracts the already updated `m_i[ii]` (= `m_{r,A∪B}`), so it computes the whole B term of Eq. (20.4) directly. p. 503 says these contributions use the max "up to the new row"; "new tile" seems intended.
- p. 499: "`B_c` rows of V" is tagged with marker 5, but in Fig. 20.8 V is marker 8 (marker 5 is the P tile). Same page: the function is called `load_KTV()` in the text but `load_KT_and_V` in Figs. 20.9 and 20.11.

- p. 510, Eq. (20.15): the middle expression is printed `2·N·h_q/(h_q + N)`; dividing the left expression by d gives `2·N·h_q/(2·h_q + N)`. The stated limit ≈ 2·h_q is unaffected for N much larger than h_q.

(20.12) stores `h_q/g_q` K/V heads, which makes `g_q` the number of query heads **per** K/V head (g_q = 1 gives MHA, g_q = h_q gives MQA). Fig. 20.19(c) shows 6 Q and 3 K/V pairs. - Fig. 20.1 call-out 5 labels the last step "S x V", while Fig. 20.2 and Eq. (20.1) multiply P (the softmax output) by V.

Simplifications or presentation choices to flag for the course (not errors):
- `d` is used for both "elements in each embedding vector" and "head dimension", and X, W_Q/W_K/W_V are drawn N×d and d×d (footnote 5, p. 483 acknowledges non-square W). §20.6 then defines hidden dimension = h_q × d. The usual distinction between model width and per-head width is therefore blurred.
- p. 481: "size of the model in terms of the number of parameters is the product of the three model dimensions" is stated without derivation.
- p. 484: inner product described as cosine similarity under a unit-length assumption "enforced with the normalization mechanism".
- pp. 479–480: tokenizer vocabulary construction is described as assigning IDs to fragments as they are encountered in training; no specific tokenization algorithm is named.
- p. 489: the new column of QK^T is described as "all zero elements"; by Eq. (20.1) the masked logits are −∞ and become 0 only after softmax.
- The chapter's "flash attention" is specifically causal, single-head, single-sequence, FP32, with fixed compile-time d, B_r, B_c, and follows FlashAttention-2's Q-across-warps split. Eq. (20.6) is the normalized merge; the code uses the equivalent deferred-division form (see §20.5 notes). How this compares in detail with the published FlashAttention papers (for example what is stored for training) was not checked against the papers.
- MLA's Eq. (20.14) uses a latent of size d per token per layer with no separate latent-dimension symbol.
- AI estimates (Eqs. 20.9, 20.11, 20.13, 20.15) count `2·N·h_q·d` operations for `(Q'K^T)V`, ignore softmax, and set p = 1 byte.
- "GB" in the KV-cache examples are binary gigabytes (GPT-3's 18 GB is 19.3 × 10^9 bytes).

Not defined in the chapter's listings: `NEG_INFINITY`, `LOG_NUM_BANKS`, `addr()`, `initialize()`, and the value of `BLOCK_SIZE` for Fig. 20.4.
