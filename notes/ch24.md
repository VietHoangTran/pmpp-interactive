# Chapter 24 — Conclusion and future outlook

- **Book pages:** 577–582  **PDF pages:** 601–606 (pdf = book + 24)
- **In one paragraph:** A short closing chapter with no code and no exercises. ## Concepts

### Chapter introduction (p. 577)
- The final chapter briefly reviews the learning goals and, rather than drawing a conclusion, offers a vision of the future of massively parallel computing and its effect on science and technology (p. 577).

### §24.1 Goals revisited (pp. 577–578)
- **Primary goal, restated:** teach the reader how to program massively parallel processors. The promise was that it becomes easy once the right intuition is developed and the work is approached the right way (p. 577).
- Two specific promises: focus on **computational thinking** skills that let one think about problems in ways amenable to parallel computing; and focus on the **factors that limit performance** of parallel applications, with a systematic approach to optimising code against them (p. 577).
- **Step one: Chapters 2 to 6.** Essential concepts of parallel computing and CUDA C++, key performance considerations for massively parallel code in CUDA, and the computer architecture concepts needed to understand the hardware limitations that high-performance parallel programming must address. Outcome: confidence in writing parallel code and in reasoning about the relative merit of alternative threading arrangements, loop structures, data arrangements and coding styles. This part ends with a checklist of common optimisation techniques that is applied through the rest of the book (p. 577).
- **Step two: Chapters 7 to 15.** Major parallel patterns proven useful for introducing parallelism into many applications. Each pattern comes with concrete code, is used to introduce techniques for overcoming common parallelisation and performance obstacles, and showcases how the step-one optimisations apply in varied scenarios (pp. 577–578).
- **Step three: Chapters 16 to 23.** Additional advanced patterns and applications to reinforce the previous step. The same optimisations continue to be applied, but the emphasis moves to exploring alternative forms of problem decomposition and analysing the trade-offs between decompositions and their associated data structures (p. 578).
- **Chapter 22's role:** within step three, it recaps the computational thinking skills that generalise earlier chapters into the high-level thinking needed for a new problem (p. 578).
- Closing claim of the section: with these insights, high-performance parallel programming becomes a reasoning process rather than a black art (p. 578).

### §24.2 Future outlook (pp. 578–580)
- **Hardware progress since the first CUDA GPU:** since the G80, the first CUDA-enabled GPU, was introduced in 2007, GPU capability has improved by 638× in computing throughput and 93× in memory bandwidth, per Fig. 24.1 (p. 578). `[device: G80 (2007) vs B200 (2025)]` See the Figures table for every number, and Unclear for an inconsistency in the 638× figure.
- These advances drove progress in HPC, AI and data analytics for science and engineering, with impact in finance, manufacturing and medicine. Chapters 19 and 20 are cited as showing GPUs igniting a deep learning revolution on very large data sets, with applications in image recognition, speech recognition and video analytics (p. 578).
- **Scale-out is growing faster than single-GPU performance:** the number of GPUs collaborating over a network on a single job has grown at an even more impressive rate since 2017. The computational demands of training Large Language Models have led leading tech companies to use tens or even hundreds of thousands of GPUs in each training job. These companies are called **hyperscalers** because of the extreme scale of their data centres (p. 579).
- That growth has driven fast advancement of networking hardware and software. NCCL (introduced in Ch. 23) is described as a new communication library used extensively for training LLMs in hyperscaler data centres (p. 579).
- **Field progress since the first edition (2010):** the spectrum of problems solvable with scalable algorithms has broadened significantly. GPU use was initially concentrated on regular dense matrix computation and Monte Carlo methods, then expanded quickly into sparse methods and graph computation. Algorithms have advanced too; some presented in the pattern, advanced pattern and application chapters are significant recent advances (p. 579).
- **Not the end:** the authors answer "definitely no" to whether fast advancement in parallel computing is over, and place us most likely in the middle of the parallel computing revolution (p. 579).
- **Paradigm shift (the chapter's main thesis):** over the past three decades, major innovations went from being driven by *physical instruments assisted by computing devices* to being driven by *computing assisted by physical instruments* (p. 579). Three worked examples:
  - **Driving:** two decades ago GPS changed driving; it is primarily satellite signal sensing assisted by computing methods that determine the shortest path. Later, peer reporting and computing through phone map apps and cloud data services let drivers reroute by traffic conditions. Today's most exciting change, self-driving cars, is primarily machine-learning computing assisted by physical sensors (p. 579).
  - **Medicine:** MRI and PET changed medicine over the past two decades; they are primarily electromagnetic and light sensors assisted by computational image reconstruction, letting doctors see pathology without surgery. Today's shift to individualised medicine is primarily driven by deep learning models and computational genomics methods assisted by medical and genomic instruments (p. 579).
  - **Semiconductors:** feature-size reduction used to rely on advances in physical light sources, assisted by computing methods that enforce design rules. Advancement in physical light sources has practically stopped; feature-size reduction is now primarily driven by lithography masks that are computationally designed to orchestrate interference of light waves into extremely precise etching patterns (pp. 579–580).
- The same shift is happening in content authoring, drug discovery, and weather/climate forecasting. Computing has become the primary driving force for virtually all exciting innovations, creating an insatiable demand for faster computing systems (p. 580).
- As argued in Ch. 1, parallel computing is the only viable approach to continued growth in computing performance; that demand will keep motivating more powerful parallel devices (p. 580).
- **Named opportunity: parallelism in accessing storage data.** Historically storage access has had a very low level of parallelism. Improving it can accelerate databases and other data-intensive applications that have lagged in innovation. Radical improvements in the precision and speed of extracting relevant portions of massive storage data will likely inspire a whole generation of applications not yet imaginable (p. 580).
- **Closing:** we are in the middle of a golden age of computing; the industry will continue to recruit and reward highly skilled parallel programmers. The chapter ends with "Enjoy the ride!" (p. 580).

## Relevance to LLM-inference kernels

- Little direct relevance to kernel writing: no technique, no code.
- Useful as course framing only:
  - The three-step map (pp. 577–578) gives a ready-made syllabus skeleton: foundations and optimisation checklist (Ch. 2–6), patterns (Ch. 7–15), advanced patterns and applications including LLMs in Ch. 20 (Ch. 16–23).
  - Fig. 24.1 supports one teaching point for inference work: on-device memory bandwidth and capacity grew about 93× and 128×, while the host link (PCIe) grew 8× (p. 578). That widening gap is an argument for keeping weights, KV cache and sampling on the device and minimising host-device transfers. The inference is ours; the book only presents the numbers.
  - The Tensor Core FP32 row is the only place this chapter touches the hardware behind fast matmul; no detail is given here.
- The chapter's LLM remarks concern *training* at multi-GPU scale (p. 579), not single-GPU inference.

## Portability notes

- No APIs are used. The chapter is NVIDIA-centred in its examples (G80, B200, NVLink, Tensor Core, NCCL) and does not mention other vendors, HIP or Metal.
- Whether comparable generational figures hold for AMD or Apple GPUs is not stated in the book: `[unverified]`.
- The thesis that parallel computing is the only viable route to performance growth (p. 580, citing Ch. 1) is stated generally, not for one vendor.

## Ideas for interactives and labs

- **Progress chart from Fig. 24.1:** interactive log-scale chart of the five rows. Learner first ranks the rows by growth, then sees the printed ratios. Include a visible "as printed in the book" caveat on the compute and NVLink rows (see Unclear).

- **Reflection prompt:** "instruments assisted by computing" versus "computing assisted by instruments": have the learner classify three technologies of their own choosing (p. 579).

## Unclear in the PDF
