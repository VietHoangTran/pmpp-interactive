# Chapter 1 — Introduction

- **Book pages:** 1–20  **PDF pages:** 29–48 (pdf = book + 28). Book p. 19 is blank; book p. 20 is the "Part 1: Fundamental concepts" divider page. The Preface (book pp. xvii–xxvi, PDF 17–26) and Acknowledgments (p. xxvii, PDF 27) are folded in below under "Preface".
- **In one paragraph:** A motivation and orientation chapter with no code and no exercises. It explains why the industry moved from ever-faster single CPUs to multi-core CPUs and many-thread GPUs, why CPUs (latency-oriented) and GPUs (throughput-oriented) are built differently, and why real applications should use both. It defines speedup, works through Amdahl's Law with numbers, names the four recurring difficulties of parallel programming (work efficiency, memory-boundedness, input sensitivity, synchronization overhead), positions CUDA against OpenMP, MPI and OpenCL, states the book's three goals, and walks through the chapter-by-chapter plan. Afterwards a reader can estimate the ceiling on speedup for a partly parallel application, explain where a GPU helps and where it does not, and navigate the rest of the book.

## Concepts

### Preface (pp. xvii–xxvi)

**Context and audience (p. xvii)**
- This is the 5th edition; the 4th edition is dated 2022 (p. xvii).
- Framing since the 4th edition: very large GPU clusters now drive AI training and serving of LLM queries; the Preface names ChatGPT, DeepSeek and Gemini as examples (p. xvii).
- Audience: graduate and undergraduate students across science and engineering, plus industry developers refreshing their skills. Assumed background: at least basic C++ (p. xvii).
- Teaching approach: build intuition rather than formalism, using CUDA on NVIDIA GPUs. The Preface claims more than one billion such processors in use and millions of CUDA programmers (p. xvii).
- Hardware/software that changed since the 4th edition: three new GPU architecture generations (Ada Lovelace, Hopper, Blackwell) and CUDA 11 through CUDA 13 (pp. xvii–xviii).

**Four new chapters (pp. xviii–xix)**
- Ch. 12 Filtering: unstable and stable filtering. Unstable filter is used to introduce coalescing of atomic operations, the warp voting functions behind it, and coalesced groups from the cooperative groups API. Stable filter is a use case for scan. It also exercises several Ch. 6 checklist items: privatization, thread coarsening, and packing in shared memory to improve memory access coalescing (p. xviii).
- Ch. 15 Advanced Optimizations for Matrix Multiplication: the Preface calls matrix multiplication the most important computation GPUs are used for today. Earlier coverage is spread over Ch. 3 (basic 2D grid version), Ch. 5 (shared-memory tiling) and Ch. 6 (thread coarsening). The new chapter adds: thread coarsening to enlarge output tiles and raise arithmetic intensity; register tiling of input and output tiles; vector loads and stores; coalesced storing of output tiles; eliminating bank conflicts; tolerating low occupancy with software pipelining. It also surveys hardware support: tensor cores, tensor memory, asynchronous memory copies, and the tensor memory accelerator (p. xviii).
- Ch. 16 Wavefront Parallelism: extracting data parallelism from dynamic programming, with Smith-Waterman sequence alignment as the running example. First attempt uses square/rectangular tiles per thread block, which under-uses warps and hurts locality as wavefront sizes change; a "hypertile" transformation tilts the tiles so all threads stay active and locality improves (pp. xviii–xix).
- Ch. 20 Large Language Models: introduces the Transformer architecture with focus on attention over tokens. Two optimizations of parallel attention are covered: KV caching (avoids recomputation while generating new tokens) and flash attention (an algorithmic transformation that cuts the overheads of multiple kernel executions and repeated memory accesses). Attention is also used to show batching: higher throughput and utilization at the cost of per-query latency (p. xix).

**Three chapters with major rewrites (pp. xix–xx)**
- Ch. 6 Performance Considerations: the previous edition's checklist had six optimizations: occupancy tuning, reducing control divergence, shared memory and register tiling (counted as one item), privatization, coalescing of memory accesses, and thread coarsening. The new checklist adds warp-level primitives (covered in later chapters) and, introduced in the chapter itself, loop unrolling, vector loads and stores, avoiding shared memory bank conflicts, and double buffering. Thread coarsening is now presented abstractly instead of via matrix multiplication, because Ch. 15 does that in depth (p. xix).
- Ch. 11 Scan: less emphasis on Brent-Kung, more on work efficiency, and more optimizations applied to Kogge-Stone (double buffering and warp-level primitives to cut synchronization overhead; register tiling to cut shared-memory latency). Adds reduce-scan-scan and compares it with scan-scan-add on memory-bandwidth use. Fixes the single-lookback implementation using C++ atomics with release/acquire semantics, and adds multiple lookback and decoupled lookback (pp. xix–xx).
- Ch. 23 Multi-GPU Programming: running example changed from a 3D stencil with non-CUDA-aware MPI to a 2D stencil with residual computation, used to introduce three models: CUDA-aware MPI, NCCL and NVSHMEM (p. xx).

**Eight chapters and one appendix substantially updated (pp. xx–xxi)**
- Ch. 4: examples now use Hopper H100 specifications; adds thread block clusters (a new level of the CUDA thread hierarchy letting more threads synchronize and reach each other's shared memory); brief introduction of warp-level primitives (p. xx).
- Ch. 5: H100 specifications; adds distributed shared memory; more thorough treatment of arithmetic intensity, compute- vs memory-boundedness, and "speed of light" analysis, which later chapters (7, 8, 11) build on (p. xx).
- Ch. 9 Histogram: running example changed from a text histogram to an image histogram; uses the higher-level CUDA C++ atomics API, which exposes scope (block, device, system) and memory ordering (relaxed, release, acquire, sequential). Code in other chapters was also moved to this API (p. xx).

### Chapter opening (pp. 1–3)
- Applications have always wanted more speed and resources than devices offer; deep learning is named as the recent example (p. 1).
- Single-CPU microprocessors (x86 from Intel and AMD) drove rapid gains in the 1980s and 1990s, reaching GFLOPS (10^9 floating-point operations per second) on desktops and TFLOPS (10^12) in datacenters. Users come to expect improvements, which funds more improvements: a "virtuous" cycle (p. 1).
- That drive slowed from 2003 because of energy consumption and heat dissipation, which capped both clock frequency and the amount of work per clock period on a single CPU (p. 2).
- Response: multiple physical CPUs per chip, called **processor cores**. To benefit, an application's work has to be split into multiple instruction sequences that run at once (p. 2).
- Sequential programs follow the von Neumann model (1945 report): execution is understood by stepping a **program counter** (a.k.a. instruction pointer) that holds the address of the next instruction. The sequence of executed instructions is a **thread** of execution (p. 2). The term is defined more formally later in the book.
- Consequence: a sequential program runs on one core, and one core is no longer getting much faster each generation; the old expectation "has not been valid for over a decade" (p. 2).
- Software that keeps getting faster is **parallel** software, where several threads cooperate. The shift is called the *concurrency revolution* (p. 2).
- Parallel programming was previously confined to expensive large machines and a few elite applications; now that all microprocessors are parallel, far more developers need it (pp. 2–3).

### §1.1 Heterogeneous parallel computing (pp. 3–7)
- Two design trajectories since 2003 (p. 3):
  - **Multi-core**: keep sequential programs fast while adding cores. Example given: Intel and AMD server processors with over 100 cores, each an out-of-order, multiple-instruction-issue, full-x86 core with hyper-threading (two hardware threads). Another example: an ARM Ampere server processor with up to 128 cores (p. 3).
  - **Many-thread**: maximize throughput of parallel applications. Exemplar: NVIDIA Hopper H100 GPU with hundreds of thousands of threads running on many simple in-order pipelines (p. 3).
- Peak floating-point throughput of the H100 "as of 2022": 34 TFLOPS double precision (64-bit), 67 TFLOPS single precision (32-bit), 1979 TFLOPS half precision (16-bit) `[device: NVIDIA Hopper H100]`. A server-grade CPU of the same year: "a few TFLOPS" (p. 3).
- Caveat stated by the book: these are raw peak rates, not application speeds (p. 3).
- The gap has pushed developers to move compute-heavy parts of software to GPUs and enabled new applications such as deep learning (p. 3).
- **Why the gap exists: design philosophy (Fig. 1.1, pp. 4–5)**
  - CPU = **latency-oriented design**. Arithmetic units and operand delivery logic are built to minimize latency of each operation, at the cost of area and power per unit. Large last-level caches turn slow memory accesses into fast cache accesses. Branch prediction and execution control logic reduce branch latency. All this lowers the execution latency of each individual thread, but uses area and power that could otherwise go to more arithmetic units and memory access channels (p. 4).
  - GPU = **throughput-oriented design**. Shaped by the video game industry's demand for huge numbers of floating-point calculations and memory accesses per frame (p. 4).
  - Memory matters as much as arithmetic: graphics speed is often limited by how fast data moves in and out of the frame buffers in DRAM. Games accept a relaxed memory model, which makes massively parallel memory access easier for GPUs (pp. 4–5).
  - General-purpose processors must satisfy legacy OS, application and I/O requirements, which makes it harder to raise **memory bandwidth** (the throughput of memory accesses). The book's rule of thumb: graphics chips have run at roughly 10x the memory bandwidth of contemporary CPU chips (p. 5).
  - Key cost argument: lowering latency is much more expensive than raising throughput. Doubling arithmetic throughput by doubling the number of units costs double area and power; halving arithmetic latency may need double the current, more than double the area, and four times the power. So GPUs optimize total throughput of many threads and allow individual pipelined memory channels and arithmetic operations to have long latency (p. 5).
  - Software consequence: GPU code is expected to have a large number of threads so the hardware always has work available while some threads wait on long-latency memory or arithmetic. Small caches on the GPU side reduce how often threads touching the same data must go to DRAM (p. 5).
- **Use both (pp. 5–6)**: with one or few threads, CPUs win; with many threads, GPUs win. Applications should run sequential parts on the CPU and numerically intensive parts on the GPU. This is the stated reason the CUDA model (introduced by NVIDIA in 2007) supports joint CPU-GPU execution.
- **Non-speed factors in choosing a processor (p. 6)**
  - *Installed base*: software cost is only justified by a large customer population. Traditional parallel machines had negligible market presence. GPUs sold in the hundreds of millions through the PC market; the chapter states more than 1 billion CUDA-enabled GPUs in use.
  - *Form factor and accessibility*: until 2006 parallel applications ran on datacenter servers or departmental clusters. Medical-imaging example: a paper on a 64-node cluster is fine, but MRI products ship as a PC plus accelerators; GE and Siemens cannot sell racks into clinics. NIH is said to have declined to fund parallel programming projects for a time for this reason, and now funds GPU computing.
- **GPGPU before CUDA (pp. 6–7)**: until 2006, programmers had to express computations through graphics APIs (OpenGL or Direct3D), i.e., as functions that paint pixels. This was called GPGPU (General Purpose Programming using a Graphics Processing Unit). It limited what could be written and never became widespread.
- **CUDA, 2007 (p. 7)**: not just software. NVIDIA added hardware: starting with the G80 and successors, a general-purpose parallel programming interface on the chip serves CUDA programs, bypassing the graphics interface. Software layers were redone so familiar C/C++ tools work.
- Other accelerators exist (FPGAs for networking are named); the book says its techniques carry over to them (p. 7).

### §1.2 Why more speed or parallelism? (pp. 7–9)
- Quantitative expectations stated: a good GPU implementation of a suitable application can exceed 100× over sequential execution on one CPU core; where there is data parallelism, 10× is often reachable "with just a few hours of work" (p. 7).
- Tomorrow's mass-market applications are yesterday's "supercomputing applications" or super-applications (p. 7).
- Examples the book uses (pp. 7–8):
  - Molecular biology: computational models complement instruments; larger systems and longer simulated time become feasible with more speed.
  - Video/audio coding: HDTV versus NTSC; view synthesis and high-resolution display of low-resolution video.
  - User interfaces: touch screens, 3D sensors and displays, mixed virtual/physical information, voice and vision interfaces.
  - Games: from prearranged scenes to dynamic simulation; this ability to model physics also underlies *digital twins* (accurate simulated models of physical objects for stress testing and deterioration prediction).
  - Deep learning: neural networks were researched since the 1970s but impractical because training needed too much labeled data and computation. The internet supplied labeled images and GPUs supplied throughput; fast adoption in computer vision and NLP since 2012.
- Common property: all these model a physical, concurrent world with enormous amounts of data, so much of the work can be done on different parts of the data in parallel, with results reconciled at some point. Managing data delivery well has a large effect on achievable speed (pp. 8–9).
- The book's aim is to make these data-management techniques intuitive to developers without a CS/CE background, with CUDA as the practical vehicle (p. 9).

### §1.3 Speeding up real applications (pp. 9–11)
- **Speedup** of system A over system B = (time on B) / (time on A). Example: 10 s on A, 200 s on B gives 200/10 = 20× (p. 9).
- **Amdahl's Law**, shown through numbers rather than a formula (p. 9):
  - Parallelizable fraction 30%, that part sped up 100×: total time falls by at most 29.7%, so overall speedup is about 1/(1 − 0.297) = 1.42×.
  - Same 30% with infinite speedup: at most 30% saved, overall no more than 1.43×.
  - Parallelizable fraction 99%, sped up 100×: time drops to 1.99% of the original, overall about 50×.
  - Statement of the law as the book gives it: the achievable speedup can be severely limited by how much of the application is parallelizable.
  - Our formalization (not printed in the book): with parallel fraction p and speedup s on that fraction, overall speedup = 1 / ((1 − p) + p/s). The book's numbers match this.
- Speedups over 100× have been reported, but typically only after optimization and algorithm changes that put more than 99.9% of the work in the parallel portion (pp. 9–10).
- **Memory bandwidth as the practical limit (p. 10)**: a straightforward parallelization often saturates DRAM bandwidth and yields only about 10×. Getting past this means transforming the code to use specialized on-chip GPU memories so far fewer accesses go to DRAM, and then further optimizing around limits such as small on-chip memory capacity. Teaching these optimizations is called an important goal of the book.
- **Fairness to the CPU (p. 10)**: a speedup figure also reflects how well the CPU suits the task. Most applications have parts better run on the CPU; GPU code should complement CPU execution.
- Current state as the book puts it: mass-market systems combining multi-core CPUs and many-core GPUs have brought terascale computing to desktops and exascale computing to clusters (p. 10).
- **Peach analogy (Fig. 1.2, pp. 10–11)**:
  - The pit = sequential portions. Hard to parallelize ("biting into the pit"); CPUs handle them well. They can be a large share of the code but usually a small share of execution time in super-applications.
  - The flesh ("peach meat") = data-parallel portions, easy to parallelize.
  - Early GPGPU interfaces covered only a small piece of the flesh; CUDA is designed to cover much more; models and hardware keep evolving to cover more still.

### §1.4 Challenges in parallel programming (pp. 11–12)
- Opening quip: parallel programming is easy if you do not care about performance, but performance is the point (p. 11).
- **Challenge 1, algorithmic complexity (p. 11)**: parallel algorithms may do more total work than the sequential one, occasionally enough to be slower on large inputs. Problems naturally stated as mathematical recurrences need non-intuitive reformulation and sometimes redundant work. Primitives such as prefix sum (scan) help convert sequential, recursive formulations to parallel ones. The concept of **work efficiency** is introduced with scan in Ch. 11.
- **Challenge 2, memory-bound execution (pp. 11–12)**: applications limited by memory access latency and/or throughput are **memory-bound**; those limited by arithmetic rate are **compute-bound**. Memory access optimizations come in Chapters 5 and 6.
- **Challenge 3, input sensitivity (p. 12)**: parallel programs react more than sequential ones to erratic sizes and uneven distributions, which give threads uneven work. Techniques for regularizing data or tailoring assignment of data to threads appear in the pattern and application chapters.
- **Challenge 4, synchronization (p. 12)**: applications needing little cross-thread collaboration are **embarrassingly parallel**. Others need *synchronization operations* such as barriers or atomic operations, which cost time because threads wait instead of working. Reducing this overhead is a theme throughout.
- These problems have known solutions that recur across domains, which is why the book teaches through patterns (p. 12).

### §1.5 Related parallel programming interfaces (pp. 12–13)
- **OpenMP** (shared-memory multiprocessors): a compiler plus a runtime. The programmer gives directives (commands) and pragmas (hints) about a loop; the compiler generates parallel code and the runtime manages threads and resources. Designed for CPUs and extended to GPUs. Its advantage is automation and abstraction, giving what the book calls "performance portability" across vendors and generations. The book's view: effective use still requires understanding the underlying concepts, OpenMP compilers are still improving, and CUDA-style interfaces are likely needed where they fall short. CUDA's explicit control makes it a good learning vehicle even for OpenMP users (pp. 12–13).
- **MPI** (clusters): nodes share no memory; all sharing is explicit message passing. Used on systems with more than 100,000 nodes. Porting effort is high: the programmer must do **domain decomposition** (partition input and output across nodes) and call send/receive functions. CUDA by contrast gives shared memory within the GPU. Most HPC developers still need something like MPI at cluster level; support is growing through CUDA-aware MPI and through NCCL and NVSHMEM (Ch. 23) (p. 13).
- **OpenCL**: developed jointly in 2009 by Apple, Intel, AMD/ATI and NVIDIA (the reference list dates specification version 1.0 to 2008). Like CUDA it defines language extensions and runtime APIs; it leans more on APIs and less on language extensions, which lets vendors adapt existing compilers. OpenCL programs run correctly without change on any supporting processor, but usually need modification to perform well on a new one. The book says the key concepts are very similar and CUDA skills transfer with minimal effort (p. 13).

### §1.6 Overarching goals (p. 14)
- Goal 1: program massively parallel processors for **high performance**. Needs a conceptual, not expert, understanding of hardware; emphasis on *computational thinking*. The book argues hardware-aware programmers will use future tools better than those who are not; Ch. 4 covers GPU architecture fundamentals.
- Goal 2: **correct functionality and reliability**. CUDA encourages simple forms of barrier synchronization, atomicity and memory consistency, and offers tools for debugging both function and performance.
- Goal 3: **scalability across future hardware generations**. The key is to regularize and localize memory accesses to minimize use of critical resources and conflicts when updating data structures.
- Method: principles and patterns are taught through parallelizing useful applications, not in the abstract.

### §1.7 Organization of the book (pp. 15–18)
- Three parts: Part 1 fundamentals (Ch. 2–6), Part 2 primitive parallel patterns (Ch. 7–15), Part 3 advanced patterns and applications (Ch. 16–23) (pp. 15–17).
- Ch. 2 preview: CUDA as a small extension to C++ supporting heterogeneous CPU/GPU computing and the Single Program Multiple Data (SPMD) model. Six-step thought process (p. 15):
  1. identify the part of the program to parallelize;
  2. isolate the data it uses and allocate device memory with an API function;
  3. transfer data to the device with an API function;
  4. write the parallel part as a kernel function executed by parallel threads;
  5. launch the kernel;
  6. transfer results back with an API call.
- Ch. 3: multi-dimensional thread organization for multi-dimensional data. Ch. 4: compute architecture and scheduling (transparent scalability, SIMD execution and control divergence, multi-threading and latency tolerance, occupancy). Ch. 5: memory architecture and the special memories for CUDA variables. Ch. 6: performance considerations, ending in an optimization checklist reused through Parts 2 and 3 (pp. 15–16).
- Part 2 chapter roles (p. 16): Ch. 7 convolution (constant memory and caching), Ch. 8 stencil (3D organization, thread granularity), Ch. 9 histogram (atomic operations, privatization), Ch. 10 reduction (control divergence, barrier synchronization cost), Ch. 11 scan (work efficiency), Ch. 12 filter (unstable and stable), Ch. 13 merge (dynamic input data identification), Ch. 14 sort (odd-even, merge, radix), Ch. 15 matrix multiplication in depth.
- Part 3 chapter roles (pp. 16–17): Ch. 16 dynamic programming and wavefront parallelism (Floyd-Warshall and Smith-Waterman named here), Ch. 17 sparse matrices (compression, padding, sorting, transposition, regularization), Ch. 18 graph traversal, Ch. 19 CNNs, Ch. 20 LLMs (KV caching and batching named), Ch. 21 electrostatic potential map (scatter-to-gather), Ch. 22 computational thinking (ends with input-centric vs output-centric strategies), Ch. 23 heterogeneous clusters (MPI, NCCL, NVSHMEM).
- Rationale for CUDA as the single vehicle: people learn best from concrete examples; depth in one model gives footing for generalizing to others (pp. 17–18).
- Ch. 24: conclusions and outlook (p. 18).

## Relevance to LLM-inference kernels
- Little direct kernel content, but the chapter sets up the mental model every later optimization relies on.
- **Throughput-oriented design (pp. 4–5)** is why an inference kernel is written as very many simple threads and why per-thread latency is not the metric. It also explains the batching trade-off the Preface attributes to Ch. 20: more throughput and utilization, worse per-query latency (p. xix).
- **Memory bandwidth as the limiter (pp. 5, 10)** and **memory-bound vs compute-bound (p. 11)**: the book's "about 10× then you hit DRAM bandwidth" claim is the general motivation for the tiling and on-chip-memory techniques of Ch. 5, 6 and 15 that GEMM and attention kernels depend on. Mapping specific LLM operations to memory- or compute-bound is our inference, not stated in this chapter.
- **Amdahl's Law (p. 9)** applies to an inference pipeline as a whole: host-side steps that stay sequential (the book gives no LLM example here; tokenization or sampling logic would be our examples) cap the end-to-end gain from a faster matmul or attention kernel.
- **Half-precision throughput (p. 3)**: the H100 figure for 16-bit is about 30× the 32-bit figure in the book's numbers (1979 vs 67 TFLOPS). The chapter does not explain why; the Preface points to tensor cores (Ch. 15) and low-precision formats and quantization of LLM parameters (Appendix A) as where this is treated (pp. xviii, xxi).
- **Synchronization overhead and embarrassingly parallel work (p. 12)**: elementwise ops are the embarrassingly parallel end; reductions such as softmax normalization, argmax and top-k need the coordination patterns of Ch. 9–12 (this mapping is ours).
- **Roadmap for this reader (from the Preface and §1.7)**: Ch. 2–6 fundamentals; Ch. 15 (GEMM); Ch. 20 (attention, KV cache, flash attention, batching); Appendix A (low-precision formats); Ch. 10–12 and 14 (reduction, scan, filter, sort) as building blocks for sampling. Fig. P.1 shows Ch. 20 depending on Ch. 19, which depends on Ch. 7.

## Portability notes
- The chapter is deliberately CUDA-centric and argues that depth in one model transfers (pp. 17–18). It names no HIP or Metal anywhere in Chapter 1 or the Preface.
- What the book itself says about other targets:
  - Techniques taught with GPUs also apply to other accelerators such as FPGAs (p. 7).
  - OpenCL concepts and features are very close to CUDA's, and CUDA techniques carry over easily (p. 13).
  - OpenMP now targets GPUs too, and offers "performance portability" at the cost of control (pp. 12–13).
  - Programs correct on any OpenCL device still usually need retuning per processor (p. 13). This is the book's only explicit statement about cross-device performance portability.
- HIP: mapping of CUDA concepts to HIP is not discussed. `[unverified]`
- Metal: mapping of CUDA concepts (kernels, grids, device memory) to Metal compute is not discussed. `[unverified]`
- The H100 throughput numbers and the "10x memory bandwidth" rule are NVIDIA/discrete-GPU framed; whether they hold for AMD GPUs or Apple silicon is not addressed. `[unverified]`
- The picture of a GPU with its own DRAM separate from host memory (Fig. 1.1 and later Ch. 2) is not qualified for unified-memory systems in this chapter. `[unverified]`
